Math Saves Lives Igor Tamm

The Taylor Series at Gunpoint ▼

During the Russian Civil War, physicist and mathematician Igor Tamm was captured by anti-communist bandits who suspected him of being a spy. When Tamm claimed to be a mathematician, the bandit leader drew a gun and demanded:

"Calculate the error introduced when a Taylor series is truncated at the n-th term. If you get it right, you live. If you fail, I shoot you."

Trembling under gunpoint, Tamm derived the formula in the dirt with a stick. The bandit leader—who turned out to be a former math student—inspected the proof, nodded, and set him free.

Math Saves Lives Archimedes

Do Not Disturb My Circles! ▼

During the siege of Syracuse, a Roman soldier barged in on Archimedes while he was deeply absorbed in drawing geometric figures in the sand.

Attempting to save his mathematical work and life's passion, Archimedes famously yelled: "Do not disturb my circles!" right before being struck down by the soldier.

Math Saves Lives Paul Wolfskehl

Saved by Fermat's Last Theorem ▼

Despondent over a romantic rejection, German industrialist Paul Wolfskehl planned to end his life precisely at midnight. To pass his remaining hours, he went to his library and began reading Ernst Kummer's work on Fermat's Last Theorem.

Spotting a flaw in Kummer's logic, he eagerly worked through the night attempting to fix the proof. By dawn, his proof attempt had failed—but he realized his passion for mathematics completely outweighed his urge to die.

In deep gratitude to the problem that saved his life, he rewrote his will to establish the famous 100,000-mark Wolfskehl Prize for whoever could finally solve Fermat's Last Theorem!

Famous Margin Pierre de Fermat

The Ultimate Historical Troll (and Amateur Lawyer) ▼

Pierre de Fermat wasn't even a professional mathematician—he was a full-time magistrate and lawyer who did mathematics purely for fun as an "amateur."

In 1637, Fermat wrote his famous equation (an + bn = cn) in the margin of a textbook, adding:

“I have discovered a truly marvelous proof of this, which this margin is too narrow to contain.”

He died without ever recording the proof, leading mathematicians on a 358-year wild goose chase until Andrew Wiles finally solved Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994 using advanced modern techniques Fermat could never have known.

Math Saves Lives Sophie Germain & Carl Friedrich Gauss

Saving Gauss via High-Society Connections ▼

When Napoleon’s army invaded Prussia, French mathematician Sophie Germain feared for Carl Friedrich Gauss’s life, remembering Archimedes' tragic fate.

Using her family's personal connection with a French general, she sent troops to personally check on Gauss and guarantee his safety. When Gauss found out who saved him, he was shocked to discover that his brilliant mathematical correspondent "Monsieur LeBlanc" was actually a woman!

Her pioneering work on elasticity and geometry also contributes to modern geometry like the Pizza Theorem (cutting pizza evenly).

Group Theory Évariste Galois

The Midnight Genius & Fatal Duel ▼

At just 20 years old, French mathematician Évariste Galois invented Group Theory (Abstract Algebra). After getting entangled in political and romantic rivalries, he was challenged to a duel over a woman at dawn.

Knowing he was likely to die, Galois spent his entire final night feverishly writing down his mathematical ideas in a frenzy, scribbling "I have no time!" in the margins. He was killed the next morning, but his last-minute notes transformed abstract algebra forever.

Professor Charles Lee told us this story and joked: "Be an algebraist and you will get a girlfriend."

Rivalry & Genius Sir Isaac Newton

Pandemic Genius & Rigged Investigation ▼

During the Great Plague, Newton stayed home in quarantine and invented calculus, optics, and the law of universal gravitation! He was so absent-minded while thinking about math that he famously tried to boil his pocket watch while holding an egg.

Newton was also extraordinarily arrogant, believing no woman was qualified to be his girlfriend because he thought everyone was stupid. When Newton and Gottfried Leibniz disputed who invented calculus first, the Royal Society formed an "impartial committee" to investigate.

The report completely ruined Leibniz’s reputation. The catch? Newton secretly wrote the entire report himself, appointed his own friends to the committee, and published an anonymous review praising his own paper!

Millennium Prize Grigori Perelman

The Hermit Who Declined a Million Dollars ▼

In 2002, Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman solved the Poincaré Conjecture, a legendary problem that had stumped mathematicians for over a century.

When offered both the Fields Medal and the $1,000,000 Millennium Prize, Perelman refused both. Declaring that the official math community was unethical, he abandoned academia completely and chose to live quietly in Saint Petersburg, gathering mushrooms in the forest and playing violin with his mother.

Secret Studies Blaise Pascal

The Devout Prodigy ▼

Young Pascal's tax-collector father forbade him from studying math, fearing he would become obsessed—so Pascal studied it in secret! He later built the Pascaline (one of the earliest mechanical calculators) to help his father with tax work.

Pascal went on to popularize Pascal's Triangle and formulated Pascal's Wager, using expected value to argue whether a rational person should believe in God.

Prince of Mathematicians Carl Friedrich Gauss

Master of Triangulation ▼

A master of land surveying and triangulation, Gauss proved at just 19 years old that a regular 17-gon (heptadecagon) could be constructed using only a straightedge and compass.

He later mentored Bernhard Riemann, and many lunar craters on the Moon are named in his honor.

Tragedy Georg Cantor

The Architect of Infinities ▼

Georg Cantor proved that some infinities are strictly larger than others, fundamentally revolutionizing set theory. However, contemporary mathematicians ridiculed his work, calling him a "corrupter of youth" and a scientific charlatan.

Overwhelmed by relentless hostility and deep clinical depression, Cantor spent his final years in sanatoriums, dying isolated in a mental hospital. Decades later, David Hilbert famously remarked: "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created."
